## Old Attacks, New Bill
The [ACHP rewrites]({% post_url 2026-08-01-Section 106 %}) to the regulations governing historic preservation reviews in the U.S. are still being debated, with inputs from various stakeholders. In the meantime, a new threat to preservation and archaeology has been raised by Senators Capito (R-WV), Lee (R-UT), Whitehouse (D-RI), and Heinrich (D-NM). The bill, entitled "Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act" has been promoted as streamlining permitting processes and promoting development. Unfortunately, it will do anything but, while harming the real progress we have made in protecting our heritage over the past 50+ years.

The bill offers token reforms while neutering much of the parts of the National Historic Preservation Act that have enabled historic preservation in this country since the law was enacted. The law is sloppily written, with multiple undefined terms, and would codify many of the poor choices ACHP has already suggested. It is a mockery of the entire idea of historic preservation, and should not become law. I am not going to get too "into the weeds" on this, but I'd like to compare some of these legislative changes with the previously proposed regulatory changes, to show why they are the same, and should be similarly resisted.

## Changes to Consultation
The ACHP-proposed rule deletes meaningful consultation, replacing it with a top-down certification process. The bill does not go quite this far, but limits the definitions of projects subject to Section 106 ("undertakings"), and changes how adverse effects are defined. This has a similar effect, significantly reducing the amount of review on projects impacting our shared cultural heritage.

## Reducing Stakeholder Inputs
ACHP proposes to limit input from State and Tribal Historic Preservation Officers, and the general public. The bill similarly reduces these inputs by adding additional ways to invalidate these officers, changing the definition of Traditional Cultural Properties, and reducing timelines for review.

The bill also provides extra opportunities for judicial relief for undertakings, and less for opponents. This adds additional cooling effect on stakeholder inputs.

## Reducing ACHP Input
The proposed legislation effectively neuters the ACHP -- which may seem like a good thing at the moment. But this also makes public input to the entire process of historic preservation *harder*, since then our only inputs will by through elected congresspeople. Recent history has clearly shown the need for pragmatic voting, reducing our choices in punishing representatives for poor choices in one area when the only alternatives are immeasurably worse.
