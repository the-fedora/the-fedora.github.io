## An Open Letter
Sens. Heinrich and Whitehouse:

I am an archaeologist from Rhode Island, and I work extensively in New Mexico. I am writing this letter to urge you both to withdraw your co-sponsorship of S. 5653, the so-called Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act. The bill as written will decimate protections for cultural and natural resources under the National Historic Preservation Act, the National Environmental Policy Act, and others. It will also negatively impact Native American Tribal sovereignty and agency, and in spite of its posturing as a "jobs" bill it will negatively impact the livelihoods of 10s or even 100s of thousands of professionals in cultural resource management, and environmental compliance.

Your bill panders to Mike Lee's weird obsession with removing cultural and environmental protections and deleting public land. It will allow the most corrupt administration in memory - perhaps the most corrupt ever - to force through projects destroying our cultural and national heritage with zero accountability or recourse. We can already see where that will lead: the Trump administration has waived NHPA and NEPA reviews for their asinine border wall, [destroying geoglyphs](https://www.outsideonline.com/outdoor-adventure/environment/border-wall-damages-ancient-arizona-geoglyph/) that stood for thousands of years, and [desecrating Native American burial grounds](https://www.propublica.org/article/border-wall-remains-native-american-organ-pipe), dumping remains with zero consideration and failing to even notify appropriately for days.

The central argument for the bill appears to be a critical need for reform in permitting processes to allow green infrastructure and other projects to proceed faster. Leaving aside the flawed assumption that the law won't just be used to ram through dangerous and environmentally damaging fossil fuel projects, the underlying assumption that delays to infrastructure projects stem mainly from cultural and natural resource reviews is fundamentally incorrect. [Chuck Niquette](https://www.facebook.com/chuck.niquette/posts/pfbid02A4x2BmL1RvtchFzRaoXtEQ3KuZDa7SYXKcDX7SckDyP6VqmdyKB4JBNeZTExZFfel) wrote a useful review of a few examples that are trotted out to push this narrative, including the SunZia, Boardman to Hemingway, and Cape Wind projects. The first two had negligible delays from cultural reviews, with much greater delays coming from planning and engineering issues. The last one ultimately failed due to poor financial management. I can speak from a place of experience regarding SunZia - when I worked with Chronicle Heritage, I was the Field Director for the cultural resource survey on NM State land for that project.

SunZia was a wind farm and transmission line project, first proposed in 2006, and it gained initial approvals in 2016. It was was pretty much immediately mismanaged. The original project proponents missed key deadlines for reviews, leading to those reviews restarting, and then key investors/anchor tenants backed out. None of that was due to cultural compliance, and better management on the project could have prevented those delays. Eventually, Pattern took over the whole project, and managed to beat it into some kind of shape. By this point seven years had passed. Our Albuquerque office won the contract for survey on State land in 2023 (we also had contracts for some related work). We were given an initial area to survey, but the team in charge of routing the project had an odd parametric design process, which meant that any change in the route propagated up and down the line. This created dozens of more new, small areas to survey scattered across the state any time the engineering team decided to adjust the route of the transmission lines... which usually happened just after we found a site. The final parts of that survey, I was often driving across half the state to put eyes on a few dozen areas smaller than my apartment. A few CAD constraints could've saved much of that effort, but in spite of that our share of the survey was closed out by early 2024, and consisted of only a small fraction of the total project time.

Cultural Resource Management rarely contributes significant delays to these kinds of projects, and when it does the underlying cause is still usually poor decisions by project proponents and engineers, who really ought to know better by now. The NHPA recently turned 60, and has protected countless irreplaceable elements of our shared cultural heritage. The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act will throw all of that away in the name of easier permitting, ostensibly for environmentally friendly projects. In reality, it will undo 60 years of work protecting our tangible and intangible history, and it will likely be used to push through further fossil fuel development, destroying both our history and our environment.

We can make meaningful reforms to these permitting processes. Pressuring project proponents to get their paperwork in order in time would help, as would offering more options for extensions. Or simply offering advice to avoid making dozens of tiny changes to project footprints, to draw from some personal experience. The Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act offers no meaningful reform, and should not proceed as written. If you truly desire reform in cultural and environmental permitting, you should wait until the new Democrat majority is seated in January, and a bill can be negotiated that promulgates reform while still preserving our shared cultural and natural resources instead of this travesty.

Thank you,

Evan S. Sternberg\\
*Archaeologist, Field Director*
