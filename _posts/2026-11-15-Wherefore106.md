## What do we lose?

The map above shows every county I've done archaeology in, in New Mexico, Oklahoma, most of Texas, and Kansas. It is mostly drawn from my work records since 2013 when I began tracking my work in New Mexico for permitting reasons, though there are a few older projects. The NM-TX-OK-KS area comes from permitting requirements set by the Bureau of Land Management -- which is why Kansas is included even though I've never worked there. The map is part of a larger project to take the chart of work I use for meeting permitting requirements and convert it into a map and geospatial representation - it is somewhat misleading at the moment, because it does not show the amount of work I've done in each county (and there are points in OK and TX that do not correspond to counties), but it does speak somewhat to the volume of work across the last 13-ish years.

Archaeological surveys, testing, and data recoveries can be triggered by any number of local, state, and federal regulations and laws. But a good number of the projects on this map -- and a good number of projects I worked on that don't show up on this map -- were efforts triggered by Section 106 of the National Historic Preservation Act (NHPA). From a Native American Village in Agawam, Massachusetts, to a bison butchering station in northwestern New Mexico, Section 106 has shaped American archaeology and my career. It is not a consideration for every project, but it obtains to any project with a federal "nexus" or primary interest. As it stands on 15 November 2026, Section 106 requires any federal-nexus project to evaluate a number of potential impacts to cultural resources. This often involves what we call "pedestrian survey", where we walk across the project areas at a fixed interval (usually 15 meters between crew members), to look for artifacts, features, and sites. Ultimately this work results in a report detailing the findings, which project proponents -- the companies building the thing -- use to shift plans to avoid impacts, and reviewing agencies use to determine appropriate mitigation measures when sites cannot be avoided. It is important to note here, that consideration of impacts is generally mandatory.

The process takes a certain amount of time. There are discussions to be had about reducing that time, for sure. But it is rarely the main driver of delays to projects, and even when it is there are often underlying project management issues, unrelated to the archaeological work. Too many times I have seen *major* engineering firms treat *all* consultation as a trivial matter they don't need to budget for at all. Which, predictably leads to failing very simple requirements for compliance -- e.g., skipping talking with Tribes, or failing to schedule archaeological surveys, all of which could have been done while planning and waiting on other necessary permits, essentially costing no time at all. Sixty years since NHPA became law, finding these kinds of efficiency boosts should be easy, but instead we are seeing a push to simply remove any consideration of cultural resources, natural resources, or tribal sovereignty, and labeling that is "permitting reform".

**What do we gain?** The authors of the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act (BAAJA) claim it will promote green energy development, and further claim this need is too urgent to push off any legislation for the few months before Democrats take control of both the House and the Senate. Meanwhile, Republican "poison pills", no doubt orchestrated by enemy of public land and conservation Mike Lee, mean that this legislation will shift the entirety of assessing impacts to cultural or natural resources to the federal agencies. The same agencies are given a broad remit to ignore any impacts if they deem "other considerations" are paramount. These could include the pressing need to increase green energy development and stymie climate change. But they could also include the "pressing" need for Donald Trump's hand-picked agency heads to push dirty energy development and line their own pockets in the process.

**So, what do we lose?** Certainly, we lose the incalculable contribution to human understanding from exploring the many sites I and my compatriots get to examine due to the existing laws and regulations. We lose a billions-dollar industry supporting this exploration. A few years ago, about 15,000 people were estimated employed in Cultural Resource Management archaeology. An order of magnitude more on the natural resources side -- conservatively(!) this bill threatens half a million jobs, careers people have spent decades building and/or will spend decades building. Bill proponents claim low 100s of thousands of jobs will be created, many temporary -- you do the math. But my career, and the careers of half a million of my friends is not the biggest thing we will lose with this act. We lose public input and voice in the development of our own communities. Any consideration of impacts to your local cemetery, the endangered bird living on the abandoned corner lot, concerns of tribes and native groups all become optional under this bill. And *post-facto* reconsideration is also limited. The first amendment to the Constitution of the United States guarantees the right to petition the Government for a redress of grievances. This law explicitly curtails that fundamental right, all while claiming to be for the promotion of clean energy while simply offering a way to push dirty energy and silence We The People.

BAAJA guts cultural and natural resource protections fought for for decades, and cuts the voice of the people out of the discussion. I doubt anyone who works in cultural resource management disagrees that reforms can and should be made. But this is not the way. Anyone in Congress who wants to actually reform these permitting processes should oppose this bill, and support new legislation developed with input from all stakeholders, including local communities, Native American tribes, Alaskan and Hawaiian Natives, and the archaeologists and other professionals who work with the current regulations and processes. Democrats have a majority in the House and Senate in the incoming Congress, they do not need to pander to industry shills and Republicans who think historic and natural preservation should just go away. BAAJA should be pulled immediately, or at least the titles and sections that break NHPA, NEPA, the Clean Water Act, and other fundamental conservation laws should be pulled.

**Contact your senators. Contact your representatives. The conservation-related provisions must go, and we can probably get a much better law overall if we just wait until January.**

**BAAJA delenda est.**
