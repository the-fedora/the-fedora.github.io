## Walker Ridge, Gulf of Mexico, Mount Denali, and Lake Ontario
What do the above four toponyms (place names) have in common? All four are places within or partly-within the territorial boundaries of the United States, and have been renamed by Executive Order of U.S. presidents. Walker Ridge was renamed to Molok Luyuk, Gulf of Mexico became Gulf of America, Mount Denali - Mount McKinley, and Lake Ontario most recently was renamed Lake America. Two presidents, three years, four toponyms. Why this is significant may require a little explanation.

## BGN and GNIS
This post began with a simple observation and question - in the latest spat with Canada over a multitude of unhinged complaints, Donald Trump issued an [executive order](LINK) on **DATE** renaming Lake Ontario to Lake America. Not long after, I began seeing social media posts screencapping [Google Maps](LINK) having already updated the name, at least for U.S. users. Google tends to use official sources for toponyms, so I wondered if those sources had been updated, or if Google was "complying in advance." As it turns out, the official U.S. government sources *were* updated shortly after the EO, so while Google's choice was still questionable, it was at least not complying in advance.

The official source of toponyms for the U.S. government is the [Geographic Name Information System (GNIS)](LINK). This was established in **DATE**, as part of the modernization of U.S. geospatial intelligence after World War II, and the names are curated by the [Board of Geographic Names (BGN)](LINK). Accordingly, all **NUMBER** domestic toponyms in GNIS are given a designation as "official" or "unofficial," and a note on the authority under which a name has been assigned. There five designations for the authority an name has been assigned under:
- Board Decision
- Congressional Legislation
- Executive Order
- Secretarial Order
- Undefined

The vast majority of these names (900,000+) are marked as 'Undefined' authority, mainly names no one has needed to redefine. Odds are, if you talk to a local about these places, they will recognize the name from GNIS, and it has "always" been named that. About 6 percent of the names (55,000+) have been assigned on the authority of the Board of Geographical Names -- these names apparently needed some official disambiguation, and that is part of the reason the Board exists. Several hundred (~600) have been assigned by Secretarial Order (other official disambiguation), and 81 by legislation. Only the four places named in the heading of this section have been designated under executive order -- 0.000004 percent. All four executive orders occurred within the past three years. Molok Luyuk was renamed under President Biden, the other three by Trump.

## Walker Ridge/Molok Luyuk
Molok Luyuk means **TRANSLATION**, and was a traditional name for the landform also known as Walker Ridge in **COMMUNITY**, California. In May of 2024, President Biden issued [Executive Order XXXXXX](LINK) expanding **NATIONAL MONUMENT** to include the ridge, naming it by that ancestral name but noting it was also known as Walker Ridge. This name was updated in GNIS, and is now the official designation on U.S. government maps and publications. The last edited date is **DATE**. The renaming was the continuation of a long-term effort to acknowledge Native American heritage and traditional ownership.

## Gulf of Mexico/Gulf of America
In the midst of a racist campaign of mass deportation of Hispanic people and any other non-whites, and a budding irrational trade war with both Canada and Mexico, Trump issued [Executive Order XXXXX](LINK) renaming the Gulf of Mexico to the Gulf of America. This was widely seen as petty and performative, prompting ridicule including from early internet mapping pioneer [MapQuest](LINK) offering a template for anyone to rename the gulf. This EO has largely been seen as a petty and juvenile attack on one of our closest allies and trading partners.

## Mount Denali/Mount McKinley
Denali was probably the most-recognized name for this mountain in **DATE**, when **PERSON** renamed it Mount McKinley in **honor of that president (expand?)**. This precipitated a **YEARS**-long effort to restore the name Denali, which culminated in **YEAR**. Trump reverted that in **YEAR**, then it was re-reverted to Denali under Biden, and most recently re-re-reverted it to McKinley under [Executive Order XXX](LINK) in **DATE** **CHECK TIMELINE/REVERT HISTORY**. The history of the name changes basically speaks for itself. The first renaming may not have been intended as a slight, but it was perceived as such, and the continued re-renamings to McKinley can only be read as deliberate insult. In fact, the "restoration" of the name McKinley was not only opposed by Native Alaskans but also by other locals of the state -- to the point that the [Republican congresspeople](LINK) for Alaska lobbied against the change, preferring Denali. The name change went through, multiple times, anyway.

## Lake Ontario/Lake America
The most recent renaming is the culmination of yet more unwarranted anti-Canadian sentiment from the White House, precipitated by trade disputes and (rather inexplicably) wildfire smoke. [Executive Order XXXX](LINK) was signed on **DATE** and GNIS was updated shortly thereafter. This apparently lead to Google's update that initially brought all of this onto my radar. No one asked for this change, except perhaps in Trump's brain... a place I refuse to contemplate for my own mental health.

## Final Thoughts
Four toponyms, four places, renamed from what they had been known as for decades. One of these was renamed to restore an ancestral name predating Euro-American colonization. The other three are erasures -- in the case of Gulf of Mexico/Lake Ontario, they are erasing contemporary names that preserved, however imperfectly, ancestral names. In the case of Denali, the renaming actually erased an ancestral name that had just recently been restored.

The replaced names all had history on their side. Only the renamed Molok Luyuk also has history on its side. It restores a history that was suppressed by settlers, while the other three are themselves suppression. There is a reason the first place officially renamed by executive order occurred in 2024 -- the reason is discourse. Like the many elements of our government officially entrusted to Congress, what we call things deserves debate and discussion. Molok Luyuk got a discourse, and input from some of the people who traditionally used the place. The discourse on the other three was intentionally short-circuited by the EOs. The Gulf, the Mount, and the Lake were autocratically decreed new names to fit the petty whims of a man who should be in jail. Names belong to the people, in any case -- and it seems clear [quite](LINK) [a](LINK) [few](LINK) disagree with most of these renamings. Words have power, names have meaning, and should not be allowed to be co-opted. The best way to fix this might be for Congress to pass an act confirming the renaming of Molok Luyuk, and banning presidents from unilaterally renaming anything.
